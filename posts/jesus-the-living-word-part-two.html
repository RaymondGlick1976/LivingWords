The Living Word: The Simplicity in Christ

The Word and the Spirit produce grace and truth in the heart that receives them. Neither feelings nor cleverness can counterfeit that fruit. This is how the saints stay true when the winds begin to blow.

When the Word Takes Root

If Part One named the two ditches — the Bible without the Spirit, and the Spirit without the Bible — Part Two begins where the balance begins to do its real work. Because it is not enough to say that Word and Spirit belong together. We must ask what happens when they actually meet in the soil of a human heart.

The answer is the Scripture's answer, and it is almost startlingly simple: the Word produces.

A living Word is not only read. He is received. And when He is received, He does what living things always do. He grows. He roots. He bears fruit. He reshapes the ground He occupies until that ground begins, slowly, to look like Him.

This is the claim of John 1. It is the claim of the Sermon on the Mount. It is the claim of every verse in the New Testament that assumes a genuine encounter with Christ leaves visible traces. It is also the claim that separates the real thing from the counterfeit — because the counterfeit cannot reproduce grace and truth. It can only imitate them.

The Word Made Flesh — Full of Grace and Truth

Go back again to where John begins. “In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God” (John 1:1). In Part One, we considered what it means that the Word is a Person. Here, we press further. John does not stop at identifying the Word. He tells us what the Word does.

“In him was life; and the life was the light of men.” (v. 4)

“The true Light, which lighteth every man that cometh into the world.” (v. 9)

“And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us… full of grace and truth.” (v. 14)

“And of his fulness have all we received, and grace for grace.” (v. 16)

The Word is alive. The Word gives light. The Word dwells. The Word is full. The Word gives out of His fullness. This is not a description of a text. It is a description of a Person who moves into a life and begins to do something.

And notice what John says the Word is full of: grace and truth. Not grace without truth — that would be mere sentiment. Not truth without grace — that would be mere orthodoxy. Both together. Because Jesus Himself is both.

This is the pattern of the Living Word wherever He is truly received. He produces what He is. A life saturated in Scripture but producing neither grace nor truth has not yet received the Word — only information about Him. Something has stopped short. The seed has fallen on the wrong soil.

By Their Fruits

Jesus gave us only one test for distinguishing real from false. Not brilliance. Not popularity. Not even orthodoxy stated correctly. “By their fruits ye shall know them” (Matthew 7:16).

Notice what He does not say. He does not say “by their claims.” He does not say “by their gifts.” He does not even say “by their miracles,” because the very next verses warn of those who will prophesy and cast out devils in His name and still hear “I never knew you” (Matthew 7:21–23). The test is fruit. And fruit is unmistakable.

Now compare what the New Testament says the Spirit produces — love, joy, peace, longsuffering, gentleness, goodness, faith, meekness, temperance (Galatians 5:22–23) — with what the Word produces: grace and truth. These overlap almost completely. Why? Because the Word and the Spirit testify to the same Christ. They produce the same Christ-shaped life in the one who receives them.

This gives the church a clarifying tool, and it cuts in every direction. A harsh, loveless teacher of the Bible has somehow missed the Bible. A “Spirit-led” person producing chaos, pride, and division has somehow missed the Spirit. Grace and truth together is the signature of the real thing. Everything else is pretense, however confidently pronounced.

The Ready Mind

When Paul arrived in Berea, something unusual happened. Luke records it in a single sentence that has shaped the church's imagination ever since:

These were more noble than those in Thessalonica, in that they received the word with all readiness of mind, and searched the scriptures daily, whether those things were so. (Acts 17:11)

Two things made the Bereans noble. And both of them are gifts before they are disciplines.

Readiness of mind — the Greek word carries the sense of a willing, open, forward-leaning posture. It is the opposite of the closed-handed listener who comes to the Word already knowing what he will accept. It is the gift Jesus spoke of when He said, “He that hath ears to hear, let him hear” (Matthew 11:15) — implying, very soberly, that hearing is not automatic. It is granted to those whose hearts have been opened to receive.

Can a person actually hear what he has already decided not to hear? Scripture's answer seems to be no. Pharaoh heard Moses. Ahab heard Elijah. The Pharisees heard Jesus Himself. None of them heard what was being said, because none of them came with a ready mind. The Word cannot produce in soil that has already been sealed.

But notice what the Bereans did second. They did not stop at being open. They searched the scriptures daily, whether those things were so. Openness alone is not nobility. A ready mind that will believe anything is not nobility — it is credulity. The Bereans were noble because they tested what they heard against the written Word. Even an apostle was not above the text. The Scripture was the anchor, and the ready mind was the willingness to let the anchor hold.

Itching Ears

The opposite of the ready mind is what Paul called itching ears.

For the time will come when they will not endure sound doctrine; but after their own lusts shall they heap to themselves teachers, having itching ears; and they shall turn away their ears from the truth, and shall be turned unto fables. (2 Timothy 4:3–4)

Read that carefully. The problem Paul names is not that false teachers will be forced upon the church. The problem is that the church will heap to itself teachers. Will curate them. Will select them on the basis of what feels good to hear. This is the deception that begins in the listener before it ever arrives in the teacher.

We live in the most efficient itching-ear age in human history. Algorithms do for us now what the hirelings of 2 Timothy 4 did for the wayward churches of the first century: they serve up only the voices that confirm what we already want to be true. A person can construct an entire theological environment that never once challenges him — and feel thoroughly spiritual doing so.

This is not a problem that can be solved by more information. It is a problem of the heart. A ready mind is the only cure for an itching ear, and only the Spirit can give a ready mind. The Bereans had both because the Spirit had already begun His work in them — softening the soil so the seed of the Word could do what seed is meant to do.

The Warning We Keep Ignoring

What unsettles anyone who reads the New Testament closely is the sheer weight of the deception warnings. They are not incidental. They are foundational.

When the disciples asked Jesus about the end, His very first word was not about wars or earthquakes or the return of Christ. It was this: “Take heed that no man deceive you” (Matthew 24:4). He repeats it three times in the same chapter. And the last warning is the most sobering: “there shall arise false Christs, and false prophets, and shall shew great signs and wonders; insomuch that, if it were possible, they shall deceive the very elect” (Matthew 24:24). Jesus assumes that deception will be spectacular. It will not look like error. It will look like power.

Paul echoes the warning. “Satan himself is transformed into an angel of light. Therefore it is no great thing if his ministers also be transformed as the ministers of righteousness” (2 Corinthians 11:14–15). The counterfeit does not appear as darkness. It appears as light. The deceiver comes preaching, healing, confirming — dressed in the clothing of the real thing.

Peter warns of teachers who “privily shall bring in damnable heresies” (2 Peter 2:1) — quietly, inside the church. Jude warns of those who “turn the grace of our God into lasciviousness” (Jude 4). Paul warns of a gospel so subtle that “though we, or an angel from heaven, preach any other gospel unto you… let him be accursed” (Galatians 1:8). Even angelic authority does not override the gospel once received.

This is the strangeness of our moment. The modern church has largely decided that deception is the concern of the paranoid. The New Testament assumes it is the concern of the faithful.

The Simplicity That Is in Christ

And then, in the middle of one of Paul's most anguished chapters, comes the verse that may be the quiet anchor of the whole Christian life.

I fear, lest by any means, as the serpent beguiled Eve through his subtilty, so your minds should be corrupted from the simplicity that is in Christ. (2 Corinthians 11:3)

The simplicity that is in Christ.

Mark it well: Paul does not fear that the church will lose its sophistication. He fears that it will lose its simplicity. Because deception almost always comes dressed as sophistication. New insights. Deeper revelations. Hidden knowledge. A second blessing the simple have not yet received. A reading of Scripture the ordinary believer has been missing. A method, a movement, a ministry, an understanding — always one step beyond where the childlike saint has been content to live.

The mark of the counterfeit is that it complicates. It adds. It elevates. It exalts the initiated above the ordinary. It replaces the written Word with the discovered insight, or the plain gospel with the deeper teaching, or the simple fruit of grace and truth with the more impressive fruit of experience and power.

The mark of the real thing is the opposite. It returns you to Christ Himself — plain, childlike, grateful, humbled. Not less informed, but less impressed with yourself for being informed. The Word and the Spirit, working together in a ready heart, produce a simplicity that the clever cannot manufacture and the deceiver cannot imitate.

If a teaching makes you feel more advanced than other believers, be careful. If a revelation makes you feel that most Christians have somehow missed the real thing, be very careful. If a spiritual experience leaves you less like Jesus and more like yourself — proud, contentious, impatient with the plain — something is wrong, regardless of how it felt.

The simplicity that is in Christ is not stupidity. It is the fruit of a heart that has learned to sit at His feet.

How the Saints Stay True

What is striking about the New Testament is how rarely the apostles tell believers to fight deception. They tell them to abide.

“As ye have therefore received Christ Jesus the Lord, so walk ye in him: Rooted and built up in him, and stablished in the faith” (Colossians 2:6–7). The defense is depth, not distance.

“Let that therefore abide in you, which ye have heard from the beginning. If that which ye have heard from the beginning shall remain in you, ye also shall continue in the Son, and in the Father” (1 John 2:24). John repeats abide six times in the space of three verses.

“Beloved, building up yourselves on your most holy faith, praying in the Holy Ghost, Keep yourselves in the love of God” (Jude 20–21). Word. Spirit. Love. All three together.

“Beware lest ye also, being led away with the error of the wicked, fall from your own stedfastness. But grow in grace, and in the knowledge of our Lord and Saviour Jesus Christ” (2 Peter 3:17–18). Growth is the best protection against falling.

Notice what is never counseled. Not once does any apostle write, “Trust your feelings to keep you safe.” Not once does any apostle write, “Your experience will guide you into truth.” Not once does any apostle write, “The Spirit will teach you apart from what is written.” The counsel is always some combination of the same five things: abide in the Word, walk in the Spirit, grow in grace, test the spirits, stay in the body.

This is how the saints have stayed true in every age. Not by cleverness. Not by suspicion. Not by chasing the newest move or the deepest teacher. By remaining. By searching the Scriptures whether those things be so. By letting the Living Word do what the Living Word has always done — produce grace and truth in a soul that was ready to receive Him.

A Closing Word

The Bereans were called noble because they received the Word with a ready mind and searched the Scriptures to see whether what was being taught was so. Both belonged together. Neither was enough alone. The ready mind without the anchor becomes drift. The anchor without the ready mind becomes dead orthodoxy.

The soul who has both — who comes before the Word willing to hear, and who goes to the Word to test what he has heard — is the soul the Spirit can actually teach. That soul begins, slowly, to look like Jesus. Grace and truth begin to grow where they did not grow before. And when the winds of strange doctrine begin to blow, that soul is not carried away, because he has been rooted in something simpler and deeper than the latest sophistication.

Christ Himself is the ground. His Word is the seed. His Spirit is the rain. And the fruit of all three is a life kept in the simplicity that is in Christ.
