Two Kinds of Shame: Holy Modesty vs. Toxic Shame

The wisdom of Sirach guides us through one of today's most pressing spiritual battles

The apocryphal book of Sirach, treasured in the early church tradition, cuts straight to the heart of our modern confusion: "There is a shame that leads to sin, and there is a shame that is honor and favor" (Sirach 4:21). No verse has been more relevant to the turbulent waters of church life over the past two decades. In our age of sexual revolution and therapeutic culture, we desperately need to recover this ancient distinction between two fundamentally different kinds of shame.

The Beauty of Holiness

Scripture speaks repeatedly of the "beauty of holiness" (1 Chronicles 16:29, Psalm 29:2). This phrase might seem paradoxical to modern ears—how can something restrictive be beautiful? Yet this is precisely the nature of true beauty: holiness is restrictive, but beautiful in that it represents intimacy with one at the exclusion of all others.

Consider the nature of intimacy itself. The most precious relationships in our lives—whether with God, spouse, or closest friends—are characterized not by their openness to everyone, but by their sacred exclusivity. A marriage covenant is beautiful precisely because it declares: "I choose you alone, forsaking all others." This is not oppression; this is the very foundation of love.

Intimacy is always guarded. Precious things are always reserved. When King Hezekiah foolishly displayed all the holy vessels of the temple to foreign ambassadors, the prophet Isaiah rebuked him severely:

"Behold, the days come, that all that is in thine house, and that which thy fathers have laid up in store unto this day, shall be carried into Babylon." (2 Kings 20:17)

Why such harsh judgment? Because intimate things are to be guarded. What is holy to the Lord must not be made common.

The Glory of Womanhood

We must begin with a fundamental truth: we recognize that women are indeed beautiful! Their bodies, their hair, their feminine grace—these are gifts from the Creator Himself. The Song of Solomon celebrates this reality without apology. This is not the problem, and to teach women that their beauty is itself the problem is to slander the Creator who made them.

But here lies the crucial distinction: the secular world knows this beauty as well, and they exploit it! Turn on any advertisement, walk through any mall, scroll through any social media feed—feminine beauty has been weaponized for profit, reduced to a commodity for consumption. This is what I call the shame that leads to sin—the toxic shame that either exploits women's beauty or makes them ashamed of their God-given glory.

The advertising industry has built an empire on the unguarded display of feminine beauty, training us all to be consumers rather than guardians of what should be sacred. When the culture treats women's bodies as marketing tools, it reveals a fundamental disrespect for the holiness of human sexuality and feminine dignity.

Holy Shame as Protection

In contrast, the "shame that is honor and favor" is the modesty that guards what is precious. As John Chrysostom writes in his commentary on 1 Timothy 2:9: "Modest apparel means not merely covering the body, but covering it in such a way that its glory is preserved for the one to whom it rightfully belongs."

This holy shame—what our forebears in the faith called "shamefacedness"—is not self-hatred or body negativity. It is the wisdom that recognizes beauty is so powerful, so sacred, that it must be carefully stewarded. Just as we wouldn't leave priceless artwork unguarded in a public square, so the Christian woman understands that her beauty is a treasure to be protected.

The Apostle Paul instructs: "that women adorn themselves in modest apparel, with shamefacedness and sobriety" (1 Timothy 2:9). The Greek word translated "shamefacedness" (aidōs) doesn't mean self-loathing—it means the proper reverence and awe that guards what is holy.

Nowhere is this principle more beautifully illustrated than in the historic practice of head covering. Paul writes in 1 Corinthians 11 that a woman's hair is her glory — and it is precisely because it is her glory that it is covered in worship. Read that carefully: it is covered not because it is shameful, but because it is glorious. In the presence of God, human glory yields to divine glory. The covering is not a punishment or a diminishment — it is an act of consecration. It declares that this woman's beauty belongs to a sacred order, reserved and set apart.

But Paul goes further still. In 1 Corinthians 11:10 he writes that a woman ought to have a symbol of authority on her head "because of the angels." This is no throwaway phrase. The early church, rooted in the Hebrew scriptures, understood this as a direct echo of Genesis 6 — where the "sons of God," understood by many in the ancient world as fallen spiritual beings, were drawn to the uncovered, unguarded glory of the "daughters of men." The catastrophic union that followed was not merely a moral failure — it was a spiritual breach, a consequence of glory left unguarded and unprotected.

The head covering, in this light, is not merely cultural modesty — it is spiritual armor. It is the declaration that this woman stands under authority, under covering, under the protection of God's divine order. Her glory is not exposed to every spiritual wind that blows. She is not left unguarded in a world where the battle is not merely flesh and blood. The veiled woman is the protected woman — protected not only from the consuming gaze of a fallen culture, but from spiritual forces that have always been drawn to unguarded glory.

Our foremothers in the faith understood this instinctively. The prayer veiling was never meant to say "your beauty is a problem." It was meant to say "your beauty is so significant, so holy, that it is brought under the covering of God's order." There is a world of difference between those two statements, and recovering that difference is essential if we are to practice head covering not as a cultural relic but as a living act of worship and warfare.

This is why the head covering has always been, at its heart, a sign of protection — the woman covered is the woman honored, the woman set apart, the woman whose glory is not left unguarded in a careless world. In a culture that tears the covering off everything sacred and puts it on display, the veiled woman stands as a quiet but profound act of resistance.

The Holiness of Human Sexuality

We must be clear: sex is not dirty, our bodies are not dirty! They are holy! Human sexuality and the physical form are among the most sacred aspects of all physical creation and must be guarded as such. The Song of Solomon, with all its passionate imagery, was included in Scripture precisely because physical love within its proper bounds reflects the relationship between Christ and His church.

The problem is not that bodies and sexuality are shameful—the problem is that we have lost the concept of the sacred altogether. We live in a culture that has only two categories: "shameful and forbidden" or "freely displayed and consumed." But Scripture offers a third way: holy and therefore guarded.

Marriage as the Pattern

Marriage itself demonstrates this principle perfectly. To choose one person in marriage is to exclude all others. The ancient vows of the wedding covenant announce to God, the church, and the world: "I take thee alone, to have and to hold, forsaking all others, until death do us part." Far from being restrictive, this exclusivity creates the safe space where intimacy can flourish.

As Dietrich Bonhoeffer wrote: "It is not your love that sustains the marriage, but from now on, the marriage that sustains your love." The boundaries of marriage don't limit love—they protect and nurture it.

Recovering Biblical Modesty

In our churches today, we must recover the biblical understanding of modesty—not as self-hatred or female oppression, but as the wisdom that guards what is precious. This applies to both men and women, though it may manifest differently.

For women, this might mean modest dress that honors rather than exploits feminine beauty. For men, it means recognizing their role as protectors of feminine dignity, refusing to consume women's beauty through pornography or immodest entertainment, and maintaining their own standards of dress that reflect godly masculinity.

Raising Children Who Know Their Bodies Are Holy

The theology of holy modesty is only as strong as the generation we pass it to. If we do not intentionally teach our children and youth that their bodies are sacred — not shameful, not cheap, not on display — the culture will gladly fill that void with its own destructive answers. Here are eight principles for raising children who understand and embrace the holiness of their own bodies:

1. Never tell them parts of their body are dirty.

Every part of the human body is fearfully and wonderfully made (Psalm 139:14). Teach children instead that certain parts of the body are private — not because they are shameful, but because they are sacred. The language of shame produces either prudishness or rebellion. The language of holiness produces reverence.

2. Teach them the language of holiness, not shame.

When a child wears something inappropriate, resist the instinct to say "that's inappropriate." Try instead: "that's sacred, and sacred things are guarded." Small shifts in language over years build an entirely different framework in a child's heart — one rooted in the beauty of holiness rather than the fear of punishment.

3. Practice modesty before they can understand it.

Modesty is caught before it is taught — but it must also be practiced before it is understood. A child who has always dressed with dignity carries that dignity into adolescence as identity, not as burden. And by dignity we mean simply this: the quiet, unspoken knowledge that their body is not on display for the world — it belongs to God, it is set apart, it is worth protecting. This is the ancient wisdom of Proverbs 22:6 — you train the way before the child understands the why.

4. Teach them that guarding their bodies is an act of worship, not a list of rules.

Holiness is relational, not regulatory. A child who understands they are dressing for God — as an act of love and consecration — will carry that conviction far longer than a child who is simply obeying a dress code. Rules without relationship produce either legalism or rebellion. Worship produces character.

5. Talk about sexuality early — and within a biblical framework.

This may be the most urgent point of all. If the home and the church do not give children a theology of the body, the culture will give them a counterfeit — and it will do so younger than you are prepared for. Name it first. Frame it first. Give them the biblical language of holiness, covenant, and protection before the world gives them the language of consumption and performance.

6. Affirm their beauty without making it central.

Tell your daughters they are beautiful. Tell your sons they are strong. But always follow it with the greater truth: you are an image-bearer of God, and that is worth infinitely more than your appearance. A child whose identity is rooted in being an image-bearer first will not be easily shaken by a culture that tries to reduce them to their bodies.

7. Teach boys to be protectors, not consumers.

The culture trains boys from childhood to consume feminine beauty — through screens, advertising, and entertainment. The biblical call is the opposite: boys are to grow into men who protect what is holy, who discipline their eyes and hearts not out of fear but out of honor. Teach them early that a man of God does not take — he guards.

8. Connect modest dress to identity, not just rules.

The goal is never mere outward compliance. A child who dresses modestly only because they are told to will shed that modesty the moment they leave home. But a child who has been taught why — who understands that how we dress is a statement about who we belong to and what we believe our bodies are worth — carries that conviction as their own. "We dress this way because of who we are, not just what we're told."

The Cultural Battle

We are engaged in a spiritual battle over the very nature of human dignity. On one side stands a culture that commodifies beauty, sexualizes children, and reduces human worth to physical appearance and sexual performance. This is the shame that leads to sin—either the shame of exploitation or the shame of prudishness that denies God's good creation.

On the other side stands the ancient Christian wisdom that recognizes the beauty of holiness—the understanding that some things are so precious they must be guarded, so beautiful they must be reserved, so holy they must be protected.

A Call to Honor

"Likewise, ye wives, be in subjection to your own husbands; that, if any obey not the word, they also may without the word be won by the conversation of the wives; while they behold your chaste conversation coupled with fear. Whose adorning let it not be that outward adorning of plaiting the hair, and of wearing of gold, or of putting on of apparel; but let it be the hidden man of the heart, in that which is not corruptible, even the ornament of a meek and quiet spirit, which is in the sight of God of great price." (1 Peter 3:1–4)

This is not a call to make women invisible or ashamed, but to recognize that true beauty comes from holiness—the beauty that draws others not to lust but to the Lord Himself.

Let us reject both toxic shames—the shame that exploits feminine beauty and the shame that denies its goodness. Instead, let us embrace the shame that is "honor and favor"—the holy modesty that guards what is precious and reserves what is sacred.

In a world that has forgotten the concept of the holy, may our churches be places where the beauty of holiness is not only understood but lived out, creating communities where both men and women can flourish in the dignity of their God-given nature, protected by the boundaries of divine love.
